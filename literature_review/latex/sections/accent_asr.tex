\section{Accent Robustness in Speech Processing}
\label{sec:accent-asr}

Before speech-to-text translation quality is attributed to architectural
choices such as Direct versus Cascaded design, it must first be established
that accent is itself a robust and systematic source of variation in upstream
automatic speech recognition (ASR) error. A growing body of literature confirms
that ASR systems, despite steady gains in aggregate accuracy, continue to
perform unevenly across speakers of different English varieties, and that this
unevenness is neither incidental nor confined to a narrow set of edge cases.

Large-scale audits of deployed ASR systems provide the clearest evidence that
accent effects are systematic rather than anecdotal. DiChristofano et al.
\cite{dichristofano2022global} evaluated three widely used commercial ASR
services on speakers drawn from a broad range of global English-language
backgrounds who all read identical text, controlling sentence content and
length while leaving speaker and recording differences as potential
confounds. They reported statistically significant performance gaps associated
with birthplace-related factors and age of English acquisition, showing that
accent-related error also persists among some first-language English speakers.
This finding is extended by Jahan et al. \cite{jahan2025unveiling}, who
benchmarked twenty variants of seven ASR model families, including Whisper and
SeamlessM4T, across gender, age, and accent using CORAAL, the Speech Accent
Archive, SBCSAE, and L2-ARCTIC. In their combined CORAAL--SBCSAE experiment,
they found that both speaker variability and the dialectal difference between
African American Vernacular English and General American English contributed
to the observed performance disparity. Read together, these studies show that
accent robustness cannot be reduced to a native/non-native binary; it spans
regional, dialectal, and second-language (L2) variation and must be measured
across all of these categories rather than treated as one monolithic group.

A second strand of evidence addresses how ASR systems generalise, or fail to
generalise, from the accents seen during training to the accents encountered at
inference. The AESRC2020 challenge \cite{shi2021aesrc} remains a foundational
reference point for this sub-theme: participants trained on 160 hours of speech
spanning eight labelled accents and were evaluated on a held-out test set that
deliberately included two unseen accents to probe generalisation rather than
memorisation. The organisers reported that, among their baselines, fine-tuning
a LibriSpeech-pretrained model on accented data outperformed training the same
model family only on the accented data, while the strongest submission used a
self-supervised wav2vec encoder and most participants trained a single average
model rather than explicit per-accent branches; these results show that
pretraining and shared modelling were useful within this benchmark, but do not
establish that they generally matter more than accent-specific architectures.
This seen/unseen distinction is also central to the accent-labelled data
reviewed elsewhere in this survey: Prabhu et al.
\cite{prabhu2023accentcodebooks} organise the Common Voice accent categories
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} into five seen and nine unseen accent
groups, and address the resulting generalisation gap directly at the acoustic-
model level by learning accent-specific codebooks that are selectively combined
at inference time, reporting reductions in word error rate on unseen accents
relative to accent-agnostic baselines. Considered alongside the AESRC2020
results, this indicates that seen and unseen accents must be reported
separately and that generalisation gaps can be narrowed through pretraining or
accent-conditioned adaptation, although the studies do not directly establish
that an absent accent degrades more sharply than an underrepresented seen
accent under matched conditions.

This generalisation gap motivates a broader set of adaptation and
regularisation techniques aimed specifically at unseen-accent robustness. Thai
et al. \cite{thai2026contrastive} introduced a supervised contrastive learning
objective layered onto standard CTC fine-tuning, requiring no architectural
change and no explicit accent labels, and reported relative word-error-rate
reductions of up to 25--29\% under unseen-accent evaluation on the L2-ARCTIC
benchmark, which they associated with more compact and stable encoder
representations. Closer to the architectures compared in this survey, Swain et
al. \cite{swain2026fair} evaluated Whisper and SeamlessM4T across twenty-six
second-language accent groups and found large, uneven fluctuations in word
error rate between groups for both model families prior to any intervention.
Their proposed fairness-prompted fine-tuning, combining spectral decoupling,
group distributionally robust optimisation, and invariant risk minimisation,
improved macro-averaged word error rate relative to standard fine-tuning and
enhanced fairness across accent groups.

Read together, these studies support two conclusions that motivate the
remainder of this survey. First, accent-related degradation in ASR is well
documented, systematic, and measurable using ASR metrics including WIL and
WER, and it affects both commercial black-box services and the open model
families, Whisper and SeamlessM4T, relevant to the Direct and Cascaded systems
compared in this project
\cite{dichristofano2022global,jahan2025unveiling,swain2026fair}. Second, the
mitigation studies reviewed in this section focus on the ASR stage itself,
through adaptation, regularisation, or fairness-constrained fine-tuning of the
recogniser in isolation
\cite{prabhu2023accentcodebooks,swain2026fair,thai2026contrastive}. Among the
studies reviewed in this section, none directly tests whether accent-related
ASR error propagates into degraded final translation quality, or whether that
propagation differs between architectures that expose an intermediate
transcript, such as a Cascaded Whisper-to-translation pipeline, and
architectures that map speech to translated text directly, such as
SeamlessM4T. Establishing whether this specific gap holds in the broader
literature is the task of the following section.
