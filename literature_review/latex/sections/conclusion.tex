\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This survey examined whether existing evidence explains how regional English
accent affects Direct and Cascaded speech-to-text translation. Three findings
shape the answer. First, published system comparisons do not identify a
consistently superior architecture: outcomes depend on language direction,
training resources, evaluation data, references, and metrics. Second,
accent-related performance differences are well established in ASR, including
for model families relevant to contemporary speech translation, but WER does
not measure final translation quality. Third, cascaded error propagation is
supported by both controlled transcript manipulation and analyses of phonetic
recognition errors, making accent-related translation degradation plausible
without demonstrating it directly.

The closest studies do not yet provide the complete comparison required by the
research question. Accent-level ST benchmarks either evaluate only cascaded
submissions or do not combine matched regional-English utterances, a complete
Cascaded comparator, and accent-stratified final translations. The available
data also impose qualifications. Common Voice accent labels are optional
self-reports rather than expert phonetic classifications; corpus versions and
recording conditions differ; speakers or prompts can recur; and CoVoST 2
references were created from transcripts rather than audio. BLEU and chrF
scores further depend on reference and processing choices, while small accent
subsets can make numerical differences uncertain.

The literature gap is therefore narrower than an absence of research on
accent or ST robustness. It is the absence, within the reviewed evidence, of a
controlled, matched comparison that treats regional English accent as the
grouping variable and final translation quality as the shared outcome for both
Direct and Cascaded ST. This supports the project question: \emph{How does
regional English accent affect the translation quality of Direct and Cascaded
speech-to-text translation systems?} A defensible answer requires identical
utterances and references across systems, speaker-aware accent groups,
consistent corpus-level metrics, and paired uncertainty estimates. Such an
evaluation can establish comparative associations between accent group and
system performance, but it should not attribute differences solely to
architecture when models also differ in training data, capacity, objectives,
or decoding.
