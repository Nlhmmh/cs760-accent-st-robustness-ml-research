\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

This survey examines regional English accent as an evaluation dimension for
speech-to-text translation. Its focus is a comparison between two operational
system categories. A \emph{Direct speech translation} (Direct ST) system maps
source-language speech to target-language text without exposing an
intermediate transcript. A \emph{Cascaded speech translation} (Cascaded ST)
system first applies automatic speech recognition (ASR) and then machine
translation (MT). These definitions establish the terminology used throughout
the survey; they do not, by themselves, imply that either category is more
accurate or robust.

The survey is organized around the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How does regional English accent affect the translation quality of
Direct and Cascaded speech-to-text translation systems?}
\end{quote}
The intended comparison concerns final translation quality across regional
accent groups. ASR word error rate (WER) is treated only as a diagnostic for
the Cascaded ST pathway, not as a substitute for measuring translation
quality.

The comparison matters because the two categories expose different potential
failure mechanisms. Cascaded systems can exploit mature ASR and MT resources,
but errors in a single-best transcript may pass to the translation stage;
Direct systems remove that discrete interface but depend more heavily on
paired speech--translation data \cite{sperber2020endtoend}. Existing system
comparisons do not establish a universal winner. Their conclusions change
with language pair, training resources, test segmentation, reference choice,
and evaluation measure \cite{bentivogli2021cascade,
anastasopoulos2021iwslt,etchegoyhen2022casestudy}. Aggregate comparisons
therefore cannot establish whether either architecture is more robust for a
particular regional-accent group.

Accent is a relevant evaluation variable because recognition performance is
not distributed uniformly across English varieties. Audits and challenge
studies report differences across regional, dialectal, and second-language
accent groups, including degradation on accents absent from training
\cite{dichristofano2022global,jahan2025unveiling,shi2021aesrc,
prabhu2023accentcodebooks}. These results establish an upstream ASR problem,
not a final translation result. The distinction is essential: similar WER can
produce different amounts of translation damage, while a Direct system has no
exposed transcript on which WER can be measured
\cite{le2017disentangling}. Evidence that phonetic ASR errors can reduce
translation quality makes an accent effect in Cascaded ST plausible, but that
effect must still be evaluated using matched final translations rather than
inferred from recognition scores alone \cite{sperber2019,pida2026}.

The literature was selected thematically rather than as a paper-by-paper
catalogue. Distributed searches covered Direct and Cascaded ST, ASR-to-MT
error propagation, accented ASR and unseen-accent generalisation, accented or
dialectal ST, accent-labelled and translation datasets, translation metrics,
and statistical reliability. Candidate metadata and full texts were checked
through official publication or proceedings records, including ACL Anthology,
ISCA Archive, and IEEE proceedings; arXiv works were retained when no venue
version was identified. A work was included when a member had read its full
text and it directly informed at least one theme, architecture comparison,
evaluation constraint, or contrary test of the proposed gap. Blogs, secondary
summaries, duplicates, and sources without verifiable support for the claims
made here were excluded. This process retained 27 works, including older
foundational metric papers alongside recent studies. Because a common
systematic search log was not recorded across all members, this is a focused
narrative survey rather than an exhaustive systematic review.

The survey makes three contributions. First, it connects evidence about ST
architecture with evidence about accent-sensitive speech recognition without
treating WER as translation quality. Second, it tests the proposed gap against
the closest accent, robustness, and error-propagation studies. Third, it
identifies the dataset, reference, metric, and dependence controls needed for
an interpretable comparison of final translation quality across accent groups.

The remainder of the survey moves from general to specific. Section
\ref{sec:architectures} compares Direct and Cascaded ST architectures;
Section~\ref{sec:accent-asr} reviews accent robustness in speech recognition;
Section~\ref{sec:accent-st} examines work at their intersection;
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} considers evaluation data and methodological
evidence; and Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} integrates the themes to assess the
research gap. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} states the conclusions and final
research question supported by that synthesis.
