\section{Cross-Theme Synthesis and Research Gap}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The architecture literature rules out a simple expectation that either Direct
or Cascaded ST will be uniformly more robust. Direct systems remove the
single-best transcript interface, whereas cascades can exploit separately
developed ASR and MT components and substantially larger task-specific
resources \cite{sperber2020endtoend}. Controlled and shared-task comparisons
show that the apparent advantage can change with language direction, training
data, segmentation, references, and metric
\cite{bentivogli2021cascade,anastasopoulos2021iwslt,
etchegoyhen2022casestudy}. Architecture is therefore one condition in the
comparison, not an isolated explanation for an observed score difference.

The accent-ASR literature establishes the first half of the proposed problem:
recognition performance varies across English accent groups, including between
seen and unseen accents, and mitigation reduces but does not eliminate these
differences \cite{shi2021aesrc,prabhu2023accentcodebooks,
swain2026fair}. The error-propagation literature establishes a second link:
changes or phonetic errors in ASR output can alter Cascaded translation quality
\cite{le2017disentangling,sperber2019,pida2026}. Neither strand, however,
establishes an accent effect on final translation by itself. WER is unavailable
as an equivalent internal diagnostic for standard Direct ST, and equal-sized
recognition errors need not cause equal translation damage.

The closest contrary evidence narrows rather than closes the gap. IWSLT 2024
reports accent-level ST evaluation, but all submissions to its accent challenge
were cascaded \cite{ahmad2024iwslt}. Papi et al. compare Direct, Cascaded, and
speech-enabled systems under accent conditions, but their broad benchmark does
not provide the matched regional-English design required to isolate the
question posed here \cite{papi2026hearing}. SeamlessM4T robustness studies
compare speaker or vocal-style variation rather than controlled regional
accent and do not include a complete matched Whisper-to-MT cascade
\cite{seamlessm4t2023,seamless2023}. Accent-explicit downstream propagation is
also reported outside natural speech translation, with accent treated
qualitatively rather than as the grouping variable \cite{losttranscription2026}.

The evaluation literature further specifies what a useful comparison must
control. Common Voice offers self-reported accent labels, and CoVoST 2 adds
professionally produced translation references, but their versions, repeated
sentences, speaker structure, and reference construction limit claims unless
they are reported explicitly \cite{ardila2020commonvoice,wang2021covost2}.
Prabhu et al. demonstrate speaker-disjoint and transcript-aware accent splits,
but only for ASR \cite{prabhu2023accentcodebooks}. Direct and Cascaded systems
must therefore be scored on the same utterances and references, accent groups
must preserve speaker structure, and corpus-level metric differences require a
paired uncertainty analysis rather than interpretation from raw BLEU or chrF
gaps alone \cite{papineni2002bleu,post2018clarity,
koehn2004significance}.

The evidence thus supports a focused gap: no study identified in this survey
combines controlled regional-English accent groups, matched utterances and
references, Direct and Cascaded ST systems, and final translation quality in
one comparison. The resulting research question remains: \emph{How does
regional English accent affect the translation quality of Direct and Cascaded
speech-to-text translation systems?} The question asks for comparative effects
under matched evaluation; it does not assume in advance that accent causes a
larger loss in either architecture or that architecture alone explains any
difference.
