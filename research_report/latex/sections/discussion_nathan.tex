\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Answer to the research question}
The benchmark supports a consistent quality ranking: Direct, then
Cascade-3.3B, then Cascade-600M, overall and in every accent group. Direct's
advantage varies by accent with small effects, but few accent pairs differ
after correction. Its smaller observed between-accent spread does not establish
stronger accent robustness because the spread differences remain uncertain
(Section~\ref{sec:statistical-results}). The supported answer is therefore
higher measured translation quality for Direct on this dataset; relative accent
robustness remains unresolved.

\subsection{Interpretation of the findings}
The WER results help describe the cascade comparison without fully explaining
the system gaps. Higher normalized WER is weakly associated with lower cascade
scores and larger Direct advantages. Direct also retains a lead where
normalized WER is zero (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}), so counted transcription
errors cannot explain the full gap. Matching normalized reference text does
not, however, establish equivalent information for Direct and Cascaded systems.
Direct exposes no transcript, preventing the same WER analysis for that system.

The two cascades provide a more controlled comparison within the translation
stage: their saved transcripts are identical, so their score difference arises
after recognition. The larger NLLB checkpoint narrows the gap to Direct,
including on positive-WER clips, but does not close it. This supports the
observed benefit of the evaluated MT checkpoint change while leaving training,
modelling and reference-matching explanations for the remaining gap unresolved.

The marker findings establish a separate distinction between visible output
behaviour and measured translation quality. Direct stays ahead when visibly
marked clips are excluded. Exact-string blocking changes the marker's spelling
and lowers scores in the 35-clip test
(Section~\ref{sec:earlier-diagnostic-results}), while cascade fallback also
lowers corpus scores (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). Neither tested intervention
provides a validated repair, and the marker's internal cause remains unresolved.

\subsection{Limitations and their consequences}

\subsubsection{Model coverage and training confounds}
The ranking may not hold for other languages, models or versions. The systems differ in
size, training data and objectives, and overlap between the evaluation
clips and the models' training data could not be ruled out. The results
compare systems, and Direct's lead cannot be credited to its design.
\subsubsection{Accent labels and sampling dependencies}
Groups mix several
varieties and self-reported labels may be inconsistent, so group differences
are coarse. Speaker counts range
from 41 to 527 per group, groups differ in what is said and how it was
recorded, and repeated sentences are not modelled jointly with speakers.
Accent is not isolated, so results are associations, and groups with few
speakers have wide intervals.
\subsubsection{Single-reference automatic metrics}
Each clip has one
reference, and a spot-check of 20 pairs found 3 with meaning errors. A low
score may reflect an acceptable paraphrase or a poor reference, so scores
show overlap with a reference, not translation quality as judged by
people.
\subsubsection{Exploratory tests and correction scope}
Only the Dunn pairwise
tests are Holm-corrected. Other $p$-values and the per-accent intervals are
not adjusted, so isolated significant results should be read cautiously.
\subsubsection{Diagnostic limits}
Marker checks use selected subsets and
changed decoding settings, and the text-input checks do not isolate the
speech pathway. They describe those samples only and do not identify a
cause or validate a fix.
\subsubsection{Reproducibility}
The recordings are excluded from the
repository, the full-run records lack a Git commit hash, and environment
and replay records are incomplete. Others can re-score from the archived
predictions but cannot repeat inference without obtaining the Common
Voice data.

In practice, the results are best used to compare these systems on this
benchmark and to design a stronger study, not to predict deployment
quality for any particular accent.
