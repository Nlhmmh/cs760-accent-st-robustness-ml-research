\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Answer to the research question.} In this frozen benchmark, the
three systems rank the same way overall and in every regional accent
group: Direct, then Cascade-3.3B, then Cascade-600M. Accent group is
associated with score differences within each system, but these are
smaller than Direct's lead over either cascade. Direct's lead also varies
only weakly by accent, and few accent pairs differ after correction. A
positive system gap and stronger robustness are different claims.
Robustness, as defined here, concerns the spread of group means. Direct's
spread is the smallest, but the differences in spread are uncertain
(Section~IV-B). We therefore conclude that Direct translated better on
this data, not that it is demonstrably less sensitive to accent.

\textbf{Supporting findings.} Higher normalized Whisper WER goes with
lower cascade scores and with a larger Direct advantage, but weakly and
only as a clip-level association. It does not show that recognition errors
are the mechanism, and WER cannot be computed for Direct, which has no
transcript. Direct's lead is larger on clips where Whisper makes errors,
but it remains on error-free transcripts, so recognition errors cannot
explain all of it. The larger NLLB checkpoint narrows but does not close
the lead, so changing the NLLB checkpoint does not account for the whole
gap. The literal marker \texttt{<unk>} appears only in Direct
outputs, but Direct stays ahead of both cascades when marked clips are
removed (Section~IV-E). The marker therefore does not explain Direct's
lead, and its cause remains unresolved.

\textbf{Limitations and their consequences.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{One language direction and specific checkpoints.}
    The ranking may not hold for other languages, models or versions.

    \item \emph{Model, training and capacity confounds.}
    The systems differ in size, training data and objectives, and overlap
    between the evaluation clips and the models' training data could not
    be ruled out. The results compare systems, and Direct's lead cannot
    be credited to its design.

    \item \emph{Self-reported, pooled accent labels.}
    Groups mix several varieties and labels may be inconsistent, so group
    differences are coarse.

    \item \emph{Unequal speakers, content and recordings.}
    Speaker counts range from 41 to 527 per group, groups differ in what
    is said and how it was recorded, and repeated sentences are not
    modelled jointly with speakers. Accent is not isolated, so results
    are associations, and groups with few speakers have wide intervals.

    \item \emph{Single-reference automatic metrics.}
    Each clip has one reference, and a spot-check of 20 pairs found 3
    with meaning errors. A low score may reflect an acceptable paraphrase
    or a poor reference, so scores show overlap with a reference, not
    translation quality as judged by people.

    \item \emph{Exploratory tests and correction scope.}
    Only the Dunn pairwise tests are Holm-corrected. Other p-values and
    the per-accent intervals are not adjusted, so isolated significant
    results should be read cautiously.

    \item \emph{Diagnostic limits.}
    Marker checks use selected subsets and changed decoding settings,
    and the text-input checks do not isolate the speech pathway. They
    describe those samples only and do not identify a cause or validate
    a fix.

    \item \emph{Reproducibility.}
    The recordings are excluded from the repository, the full-run
    records lack a Git commit hash, and environment and replay records
    are incomplete. Others can re-score from the archived predictions
    but cannot repeat inference without obtaining the Common Voice data.
\end{itemize}

In practice, the results are best used to compare these systems on this
benchmark and to design a stronger study, not to predict deployment
quality for any particular accent.
