\subsection{Evaluation Data and Reference Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Speech, English reference transcripts and accent metadata come from the
validated subset of Common Voice Scripted Speech 26.0 --
English~\cite{cv26release}, a crowd-sourced corpus of read
sentences~\cite{ardila2020commonvoice}. Each clip was assigned the first
listed self-reported accent (8 of the 4,200 selected clips list more than
one), and rows missing transcript text, a sentence ID, a speaker ID or an
accent label were discarded. Two groups are pooled regional categories defined
by Common Voice: India and South Asia (India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka) and Southern
African (South Africa, Zimbabwe, Namibia). Simplified Chinese references are
existing English-to-Chinese translations from CoVoST~2~\cite{wang2021covost2},
built on an earlier Common Voice release. CoVoST~2 lists each translation
under a Common Voice file name without the English text; looking these names
up in the 26.0 validated subset recovered the English sentence for 99.6\% of
its English-to-Chinese rows, and clips were then matched to translations by
normalized sentence text (lower-cased, whitespace collapsed, punctuation
removed). Where several translations shared a normalized sentence, the first in
CoVoST~2 file order was kept, so a clip's reference may have been produced for
another recording of the same sentence.

The five originally planned groups (Filipino, Hong Kong English, India and
South Asia, Malaysian English, Southern African) gave an initial pool of
45,996 clips with a reference. Groups were sampled independently rather than
matched on content; only six normalized sentences were shared by all five.
Durations were kept between the pool's 1st and 99th percentiles (1,728 and
9,384~ms), and four clips whose transcripts implied more than eight
whitespace-delimited words per second of audio were removed as implausible. Of
six candidate comparison groups filtered with the same thresholds, the two
with the largest pools after the speaker cap were added: United States and
England English (the others were Canadian, Australian, New Zealand and
Scottish English). Every group was capped at 100 randomly chosen clips per speaker before
600 clips per group were drawn (seed 760 for both steps;
Table~\ref{tab:dataset}).

The set contains 4,200 clips (1.73--9.38~s; 5.39~h in total) from 1,586
pseudonymous speaker IDs, none shared across groups, and 370 normalized
sentences occur in more than one clip. Speaker identifiers were replaced by
regenerated IDs before export; this is pseudonymization rather than
anonymization, since one speaker's clips remain linkable.

After the set was frozen, one author manually checked 20 English
sentence--Chinese reference pairs sampled at clip level (seed 760). No
reference appeared to belong to a different sentence, but 5 had minor issues
and 3 had meaning errors, for example rendering ``Cheers mate,'' said on
leaving a taxi, as a toast; 20 pairs cannot estimate translation-error
prevalence precisely. Equal clip counts also do not equalize speakers or
content: speaker counts differ more than tenfold (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}),
Hong Kong English sentences are shorter (8.1 versus 9.0--9.4 words on average
elsewhere), and male gender labels range from 73.3\% to 93.7\% of clips across
groups. CoVoST~2's train/dev/test labels were not retained, and overlap with
the pretrained systems' training data could not be ruled out. Repeated clips
per speaker motivate speaker-clustered uncertainty estimates
(Section~\ref{sec:statistics}); the remaining imbalances limit interpretation
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
