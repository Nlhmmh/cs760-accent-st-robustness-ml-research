\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\textit{Accent-sensitive recognition.}
DiChristofano et al.~\cite{dichristofano2022global} evaluated commercial ASR
services using speakers reading the same passage and reported disparities
associated with English-language backgrounds. Controlling text improves
comparability but does not eliminate speaker or recording differences.
Prabhu et al.~\cite{prabhu2023accentcodebooks} used Common Voice accent labels to
construct speaker-disjoint sets for seen and unseen accents, and learned
accent-specific codebooks to improve recognition. These studies motivate evaluating
regional groups separately. Their outcome is ASR performance, however, rather
than final translation quality; their adaptation methods are also distinct from
the present evaluation of frozen, unadapted systems.

\textit{Direct and Cascaded translation.}
Sperber and Paulik~\cite{sperber2020endtoend} describe trade-offs between
committing to intermediate ASR representations and modelling translation
directly, including data scarcity and separate component objectives.
Bentivogli et al.~\cite{bentivogli2021cascade} compared Direct and Cascaded
systems on matched MuST-C segments for English-to-German, Italian and Spanish,
using automatic scores and professional post-editing. The approaches were
similar on several human measures, while some conclusions changed with language
direction and evaluation measure. Their results support matched evaluation,
but do not imply a universal ranking or describe the regional English-to-Chinese
setting studied here. The present comparison also uses different pretrained model
families, so shared test inputs do not isolate architecture from training resources.

\textit{Recognition errors and translation.}
Le et al.~\cite{le2017disentangling} showed that ASR errors can have different
consequences for French-to-English translation: some errors produce substantial
translation damage, whereas others have little effect. Their phrase-based MT
setting limits direct transfer to current neural cascades.
Sperber et al.~\cite{sperber2019} compared translation under correct, decoded
and perturbed intermediate transcripts; the cascade was particularly sensitive
to changing those labels. These findings justify examining both WER and
target-language scores. They do not establish that regional accent is the cause of
translation differences in an observational benchmark, nor that a WER
correlation identifies the mechanism inside a Direct model.

\textit{Data and reference design.}
Common Voice provides read speech with optional, self-reported accent metadata,
rather than independently verified phonetic labels~\cite{ardila2020commonvoice}.
CoVoST 2 extends Common Voice with professional translations of source
transcripts and documented quality checks~\cite{wang2021covost2}. Together they
support accent-labelled speech translation, but they were not designed to
isolate accent from speaker and sentence content. The balanced clip counts and
matched system inputs in the present study improve comparison without removing those
dependencies or reference-matching limitations.

\textit{Evaluation and statistical reliability.}
BLEU evaluates reference n-gram agreement~\cite{papineni2002bleu}; chrF and chrF++
provide character-based and combined character/word
measures~\cite{popovic2015chrf,popovic2017chrfpp}. Post~\cite{post2018clarity}
shows why tokenization and scoring settings must accompany reported BLEU scores.
Koehn~\cite{koehn2004significance} motivates paired bootstrap comparisons on the
same test items. The present study retains paired evaluation with speaker-clustered
resampling, distinguishes corpus scores from sentence means, and reports uncertainty in accent
variation. The resulting study connects these literature themes in a matched
seven-group, three-system benchmark with explicitly exploratory diagnostics;
it does not claim that these themes are absent from all prior work.
