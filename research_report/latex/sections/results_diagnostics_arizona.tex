\subsubsection{Accent}
The group marker rates in
Section~\ref{sec:earlier-diagnostic-results} span about three percentage
points, and the speaker-clustered joint test did not detect an association
between accent and marker presence (Table~\ref{tab:further-diagnostics});
sentence clustering or length adjustment does not change this ($p=0.45$ and
$0.48$, respectively). The true between-group differences remain uncertain. Of
the 370 normalized sentences recorded more than once, six were marked in some
recordings but not others.

\subsubsection{Input modality}
With reference-transcript input, the marker recurs in
most speech-marked clips but in only 2.1\% of the 3,692 unmarked clips, so the
anomaly is not exclusive to speech input; Whisper-transcript input gives a
similar overall rate (11.9\% versus 12.6\%). Speech and reference-transcript
input still differ for 138 clips (59 marked only with speech input, 79 only
with reference-transcript input). In the
700-clip speech re-run, 694 outputs reproduced their archived strings exactly,
and none of the 74 clips with special UNK ID~1 showed the visible marker in
either run. The count of 508 refers to the visible string, not to special-ID
generation, so outputs with hidden ID~1 can remain in the marker-free subset
of Section~\ref{sec:synthesis-results}.

\subsubsection{Source wording}
Marker presence is associated with source vocabulary
that is rare in general English. The rate falls from 31.3\% (82 of 262 clips)
when the rarest content lemma has Zipf $\le 2.5$ to 5.1\% (119 of 2,323) when
it exceeds 4 (including imputed clips), and after length adjustment each
tenfold increase in that lemma's frequency is associated with about half the
odds of a marker (Table~\ref{tab:further-diagnostics}). Proper nouns show no
clear association (OR 0.95 [0.75, 1.21], adjusted for length and rarest-lemma
frequency). Among the 385 sentences with marked text-input translations,
re-translation without the selected word was marker-free far more often than
without a random other word (Table~\ref{tab:further-diagnostics}), and the
gap remains in the 161 sentences whose random word is not a spaCy stop word
(78.3\% versus 13.0\%). Selected words are rarer than the other non-stop words
(median surface-form Zipf 3.8 versus 4.6), but because each was chosen to
maximize the drop in summed marker-start probability at a single decoding
step, the deletion comparison measures sensitivity to a targeted deletion rather than
independent evidence that rarity causes the marker.

\subsubsection{Summary}
Together, these checks characterize the marker as an anomaly of the Direct
checkpoint that is associated with source wording and shows no detected
accent pattern in its incidence; its internal cause remains unresolved. Word
removal can also alter meaning, so marker disappearance alone does not
indicate a better translation. These diagnostics concern marker incidence, not
whether the score gap between marked and unmarked outputs differs across
accent groups; Section~\ref{sec:synthesis-results} examines the system
comparisons with visibly marked clips excluded.
